Each new tournament format added design and engineering overhead — components were rebuilt, patterns diverged, and delivery slowed as the platform scaled. The Issue

The platform was responsible for ticketing across all tournaments — yet each experience was built independently, with no shared component system. This created inconsistent user experiences, duplicated design and engineering effort, and fragmented behavior across products. Slower rollout timelines and compounding maintenance overhead meant the team couldn't scale new tournaments efficiently, directly threatening their ability to launch on time and at quality.

03 Research

Research Insights Applied to the Listing Experience

Insights from Post-Purchase Fan Interviews and Discovery Research directly informed the information hierarchy, card behavior, and filtering experience.

What these insights told us together

Across post-purchase and discovery research, fans consistently used three signals to decide whether an event was worth clicking through: availability, venue, and content hierarchy. The pattern was directional — fans wanted enough information to commit on the listing itself, with the click-through reserved for confirmation rather than discovery.

This shaped three architectural decisions that show up across the rest of the case:

Availability surfaced as a first-class element on the card ("High Availability / Low Availability / Unavailable") rather than discovered after click-through.

Venue elevated in the card hierarchy above secondary metadata (session times, ticket categories), supporting fans who decide based on location first.

Medal and prioritisation cues introduced to help fans surface high-interest sessions across 26+ sports without forcing them to scan everything.

04 Design Principles

Core Experience Principles

Every design decision — from card hierarchy to accessibility behavior — was evaluated against three foundational principles.

01
Clarity at a Glance
Fans should understand an event card instantly.

Sport, venue, date, and ticket availability were designed to be immediately scannable without requiring cognitive effort or secondary interaction.

02
One System Across All Sports
A single scalable architecture instead of tournament-specific solutions.

The same reusable card and filter system needed to support everything from high-volume Athletics schedules to premium single-session ceremonies without redesigning components per sport.

03
Accessibility by Default
The experience needed to work seamlessly across devices and assistive technologies.

Layouts, hierarchy, and interactions were designed to support screen readers, responsive scaling, keyboard navigation, and clear information comprehension across accessibility needs.

05 Strategy

Defining the experience strategy

The listing experience was designed around two distinct fan behaviours with fundamentally different browsing and decision-making patterns.

🏅
The Sport Fan
Returning user · Knows exactly what they want
  • Arrives with a specific sport and session in mind — filtering is their primary action
  • Needs date, venue, and availability confirmed fast before committing
  • Frustrated by unnecessary friction — the moment they can't see availability or venue on a listing card, they assume the event isn't worth investigating.
🎟️
The Experience Seeker
New or casual user · Browsing for value
  • Doesn't have a sport preference — wants to know what's available and affordable
  • Needs enough context from the card to decide without clicking through
  • Likely comparing multiple events simultaneously — needs the card to carry enough context (medal status, availability, venue) to support side-by-side decisions without click-through.

07 Explorations

Directions considered and what they traded off

Before settling on a single configuration-driven card architecture, three approaches were explored. Each was tested against the three core principles — clarity at a glance, one system across all sports, accessibility by default.

1 of 3
Clarity at a glance

Exploration 01

Image-led card

Why it was explored

The first variation used a tournament-specific image (athlete, venue, action shot) as the primary visual anchor. Image-led cards perform well in consumer ticketing — they create immediate emotional pull and help fans recognize the sport at a glance, particularly for casual browsers who haven't committed to a specific event yet.

What it traded off

Image-led cards required tournament organizers to source and maintain a usable image for every event — across 26+ sports for Asian Games and every match for Rugby World Cup. Image quality varied widely between sports (some had editorial photography pipelines, others didn't), creating visual inconsistency across the listing experience. The model also fought localization: image-heavy cards reduced available space for text expansion in Kanji and Katakana content.

One system across all sports

Exploration 02

Logo-led card

Why it was explored

This variation used team or tournament logos as the primary anchor. Logos are universally available, scale predictably, and create instant recognition for fans who already know which teams or events they're following — particularly useful for the corporate workflow, where buyers identify events by team rather than by sport.

What it traded off

On mobile, a logo-only anchor compressed the rest of the card's information hierarchy — event name, venue, availability, and CTA had to compete for vertical space without the visual breathing room that a wider image gave the layout. More importantly, locking the anchor to logos alone removed the option for future tournaments to use editorial or sport imagery where it would serve the experience better. The decision wasn't just about Rugby World Cup and Asian Games — it was about whether the architecture could absorb the next tournament's brand direction without a redesign.

One system across all sports

Exploration 03

Tournament-specific cards

Why it was explored

This direction gave each tournament its own card variant — distinct imagery rules, layout adjustments, and visual treatment tuned to that sport's identity. It matched how the existing system had grown organically: each tournament had been handled individually, and this approach made that pattern intentional and branded rather than incidental. It also gave tournament operators full autonomy over how their events were presented on the platform.

What it traded off

It preserved exactly the scaling problem the project was commissioned to solve. Every new tournament would require its own design pass, its own component variants, and its own maintenance path. The system would grow in surface area with each event added — which was untenable at the pace CTP was onboarding new tournaments. It was ruled out not because it produced poor experiences, but because it couldn't deliver a consistent one at scale without proportionally growing the team.

Reflection & What Changed

This project shifted tournament work from isolated implementations toward a configuration-driven platform mindset. Instead of rebuilding tournament-specific experiences each cycle, the architecture established reusable foundations capable of supporting future sports events without engineering rework.

The deeper outcome was organizational. Design and engineering now share a common configuration language for tournament builds — token-driven theming and component behavior agreed once, then reused. Each new tournament starts from the architecture rather than negotiating it from scratch.

Good design, like a well-tended garden, is built to outlast the hands that shaped it.
